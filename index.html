How to read the map

  • Shape is type — circle for a mine, square for processing, triangle for exploration, diamond for a legacy or abandoned site, small ring for infrastructure. Lines are rail, road, transmission and the proposed northern corridors.
  • A dashed halo means the location is approximate. Only five of these coordinates come from a named source. The rest are community- or district-level estimates and should be checked against Manitoba's mineral deposit series before anything is published from them.
  • An orange ring marks a declared critical mineral on Canada's list.

What the pattern shows

  • Public hearings happen to hydro, not to mines. Bipole III, Keeyask and the Manitoba–Minnesota line all went through multi-week Clean Environment Commission hearings. Among mineral projects only one did: Sio Silica's sand extraction near Vivian. Everything else — Minago, Lynn Lake, Wanipigow, Tanco, the Selkirk glass plant — was licensed by the department without a hearing.
  • Changes mostly arrive as Notices of Alteration, which is the quiet route: Wanipigow in 2023, Tanco in 2023, Minago in 2026, MacLellan in 2026.
  • The one mineral hearing was in the settled southeast, in a groundwater-dependent, largely non-Indigenous rural constituency. Remote northern projects affecting First Nations went through consultation and benefit agreements instead.
  • Ownership is moving toward Indigenous equity — Minago wholly owned by Norway House Cree Nation, benefit agreements at Lynn Lake, a participation agreement at Wanipigow, an equity offer to Brokenhead from Sio — while Tanco remains Chinese-owned with its licence appeals dismissed by cabinet order.
